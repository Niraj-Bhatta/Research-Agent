# Comprehensive Research Report on Nepal: History, Geopolitics, Economy, and Future Outlook

## Abstract
This technical report provides a comprehensive overview of Nepal, detailing its historical political evolution, contemporary economic framework, regional geopolitical dynamics, geographical and climate vulnerabilities, socio-cultural landscape, and future outlook for sustainable development. By analyzing archival data and current regional reports, this document evaluates Nepal's transition from a monarchical state to a federal democratic republic, its structural economic reliance on remittances and hydropower, and its strategic diplomatic positioning between two nuclear-armed neighbors, India and China.

## I. INTRODUCTION
Nepal is a landlocked nation positioned along the southern slopes of the Himalayas, bounded by the People's Republic of China to the north and the Republic of India to the south, east, and west. Its unique geographical placement serves as a critical buffer zone in South Asia [5]. Over the past three centuries, Nepal has undergone profound political transformations—evolving from a fragmented array of hill principalities into a unified kingdom, transitioning through an autocratic oligarchy and a constitutional monarchy, and finally establishing itself as a federal democratic republic [5]. Understanding Nepal requires an interdisciplinary assessment of its historical trajectory, macroeconomic indicators, geopolitical balancing acts, environmental challenges, and long-term developmental roadmap.

## II. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND AND POLITICAL EVOLUTION
The political lineage of modern Nepal traces back to the mid-18th century. Beginning in 1743, Prithvi Narayan Shah, ruler of the Gorkha Kingdom, launched a military and diplomatic campaign to unify fragmented hill states and principalities to protect the region from external colonization [5]. This campaign culminated in the annexation of the Kathmandu Valley, establishing Kathmandu as the capital of the unified Kingdom of Nepal [5]. Subsequent rulers expanded the kingdom's borders into Tibet, precipitating military conflicts with Tibet, China, and British India that ultimately defined Nepal's modern boundaries [5]. Despite persistent internal power struggles among royal factions and regional elites, Nepal maintained its sovereignty throughout the 19th and 20th centuries [5].

A major structural shift occurred following the 1846 Kot Massacre, during which hereditary Rana prime ministers seized absolute executive power from the Shah monarchs, reducing the royal lineage to a nominal role until 1951 [5]. The overthrow of the Rana regime in 1951 opened pathways to parliamentary governance, though the nation subsequently operated under an authoritarian royalist Panchayat system until 1990 [5]. A pro-democracy movement in 1990 dismantled the Panchayat system, transforming Nepal into a constitutional Hindu monarchy with a multiparty parliamentary framework [5]. 

Persistent democratic instability and party conflicts precipitated a decade-long armed Maoist insurgency starting in 1996, which, compounded by the 2001 royal massacre, plunged the state into civil conflict [5]. A 19-day pro-democracy movement in 2006 successfully restored popular sovereignty and concluded the civil war via a formal peace accord [5]. An elected Constituent Assembly met in May 2008, officially abolishing the 240-year-old monarchy and establishing a republic [5]. This political evolution culminated in the promulgation of the 2015 constitution, which instituted a federal system comprising seven provinces and defined the nation as a multiethnic, multilingual, multicultural, and secular republic [5].

## III. ECONOMIC STRUCTURE, GEOPOLITICS, AND SOCIO-CULTURAL LANDSCAPE
Nepal’s contemporary economy is characterized by uneven growth and structural reliance on external financial flows. Real GDP growth for the fiscal year 2025/26 is estimated at 3.85%, with total GDP approximating Rs. 6,600 billion [14]. While the services and industrial sectors—particularly electricity generation—demonstrate strong performance, agriculture and public administration display sluggish growth rates [14]. Approximately 20.3% of the population lives below the national poverty line, and roughly 40% of economic activity remains informal [18].

Remittances represent the dominant macroeconomic force in Nepal, accounting for a record 33% of GDP (reaching roughly NPR 2.18 trillion in FY 2025/26), which is nearly six times the global average baseline [14, 17]. These official inflows dwarf foreign aid, exports, and tourism earnings, covering approximately 84% of the trade deficit [17]. This reliance heavily stimulates domestic consumption rather than domestic productive investment, generating a "Dutch Disease" dynamic [17]. Remittance growth is further sustained by a structural shift toward higher-wage labor markets in Europe, Japan, South Korea, and Australia, alongside currency depreciations and informal channels such as *hundi* [18].

Agriculture, forestry, and fishing remain the largest sector contributor to GDP at 24.0% in 2025/26, employing the largest share of the working population, particularly in rural regions [14]. Conversely, the services sector dominates overall output at 60% to 62% of GDP, driven by trade, remittance-financed consumption, and public services [14]. The industrial sector is spearheaded by electricity generation, which expanded by nearly 21%, whereas manufacturing and merchandise exports remain minimal, accounting for only 3% to 4% of GDP [14, 17].

Geopolitically, Nepal functions as a strategic landlocked buffer state situated between India and China [5]. India views Nepal as a vital protective shield for its vulnerable Siliguri Corridor ("chicken’s neck") connecting its northeastern states, leveraging historical ties, open borders, and economic interdependence to counteract Chinese influence [5]. Meanwhile, under Xi Jinping, China pursues a more assertive strategy utilizing infrastructure diplomacy—such as the Belt and Road Initiative and the Trans-Himalayan Multi-Dimensional Connectivity Network—to secure its Tibetan border and gain access to South Asian markets [5]. Both regional powers compete intensely over hydropower investments and cross-border railway infrastructure proposals [5]. To protect its sovereignty, Nepal employs a foreign policy strategy of strategic hedging and non-alignment [5].

Socio-culturally, Nepal exhibits deep multi-ethnic diversity, officially recording 142 castes and ethnicities and 124 distinct mother tongues in the 2021 census [38]. Major ethnic groups include the Kshetri, Brahman-Hill, Magar, Tharu, Tamang, Bishwokarma, Musalman, and Newar [38]. Demographics generally align with topographical elevations, ranging from Tibetan-descent communities in the northern alpine peaks to Indo-Aryan and indigenous groups in the hills and southern Terai plains [38]. Religiously, the population is predominantly Hindu (81.2%), followed by Buddhists (8.2%), Muslims (5.1%), Kirats (3.2%), and Christians (1.8%), featuring a deep syncretism of Hindu, Buddhist, animist, and Tantric traditions [38]. Nepal's cultural heritage is globally underscored by UNESCO World Heritage sites, including the multi-monument zones of the Kathmandu Valley and the sacred birthplace of Lord Buddha in Lumbini [38].

## IV. GEOGRAPHY, ENVIRONMENT, AND FUTURE OUTLOOK
Nepal faces severe geological and environmental vulnerabilities. Positioned along the active boundary where the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates collide at approximately 5 cm per year, the country lies within seismic zone V, rendering it the 11th most earthquake-prone nation globally [35]. Its fragile topography—characterized by steep slopes, narrow valleys, and deep clay deposits in the Kathmandu Valley prone to liquefaction—amplifies risks from secondary hazards such as landslides, rockfalls, and flash floods [35]. Globally, Nepal is ranked as the 20th most multi-hazard-prone country and the 4th most vulnerable to climate change hazards [35].

Climate change impacts are acutely visible in the Himalayas, where rapid air temperature increases are driving extreme glacial recession and ice mass loss [32]. Nepal lost nearly one-third of its ice mass over a thirty-year span, with glacial melt accelerating significantly in the past decade [32]. This melting has caused glacial lakes to multiply from 606 in 1977 to 1,541 by 2017, dramatically escalating the risk of Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs), downstream inundation, and slope destabilization [32, 35]. Furthermore, shifting precipitation patterns have caused reduced snowfall, irregular monsoon rainfall, and intensified droughts and riverbank erosion across the southern Terai and hilly regions [35].

Looking toward the future, Nepal has integrated the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development into its national frameworks [52]. Having achieved an average midpoint progress of approximately 41 percent, the country is projected to attain roughly 60 percent of its Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030, requiring an estimated cumulative investment of US$ 163 billion [52]. 

To catalyze economic transformation and achieve carbon neutrality by 2045, Nepal has prioritized the hydropower sector as a primary engine for development [52]. The national Energy Development Roadmap aims to expand total capacity to 10,000 MW by 2026—effectively ending dry-season electricity imports and transitioning to an exclusive power exporter—and target 28,500 MW by 2035 [52]. Realizing these targets requires overcoming structural barriers, including run-of-river seasonal imbalances, high capital costs, climate vulnerabilities, complex regulatory hurdles, and regional geopolitical sensitivities [52]. Sustaining these developmental advancements will ultimately depend on navigating political stability, reinforcing institutional frameworks, and securing long-term foreign direct investment and climate financing [52].
